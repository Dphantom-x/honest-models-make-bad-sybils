Ask, then count. Every one of 59 live AI swarms that attacked a simulated model hub either admitted it was one operator (36) or lied and was flagged within an hour by a test on its own downloads (23). We study what a victim can do with only its own logs, after the July 2026 Hugging Face incident. We gave live models from three providers a goal one account’s quota cannot meet: download a whole access-controlled dataset. Every attacking model made the same Sybil move, one operator posing as many users: a dozen or more accounts, each taking a different slice. Logs alone cannot tell such a swarm from an honest team splitting the same work. So the hub asks one access-form question: is this account used together with others? All 19 swarms in our main test said yes, and 14 of 15 still did without the brief’s test framing. For accounts that say no, it counts collisions: unrelated people who each take a few pieces of a dataset overlap, like shared birthdays in a room; a planned split rarely does. Swarms lied mostly when their operator told them to or when honesty cost them access, and every liar was flagged. An attacker who knows the test can defeat its simplest form, and a stress test of our own on a real 1995 web log found the simulated cutoffs too low, so each hub must set its own. Hugging Face’s access forms already support the question.

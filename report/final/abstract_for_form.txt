Asked once on an access form, AI swarms admitted they were one operator: all 19 in our main test, usually listing their other accounts. When swarms lied, their downloads gave them away. We study what a victim can do with only its own logs, after the July 2026 Hugging Face incident. In a simulated model hub with swarm-like honest groups, we gave live models from three providers a goal one account’s quota cannot meet: download a whole access-controlled dataset. Gemini, DeepSeek, Claude Opus 4.8 and Sonnet 4.6 all made the same Sybil move, one operator posing as many users: a dozen or more new accounts, each taking a different slice. A short rule for new accounts splitting one dataset caught all 58 attack episodes, but an honest team doing the same work looks identical. So we add two cheap steps. First, ask: with the brief’s test framing removed, 14 of 15 swarms still admitted it. Second, count collisions: unrelated people who each take a few pieces of a dataset overlap, like shared birthdays in a room; a planned split rarely does. When their operator told them to deny, or when the hub refused anyone who said yes, most swarms lied, and the collision test flagged all 23 lying swarms within an hour. An attacker who knows the test can defeat its simplest form; a coverage version still catches such swarms, with more false alarms. Hugging Face’s access forms already support the question.
